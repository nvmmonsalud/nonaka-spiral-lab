# Theory Brief: Nonaka & Takeuchi's Knowledge-Creation Theory — SECI, Ba, and the Knowledge Spiral

> **Phase 0 output** — theoretical foundation for *Knowledge Creation in the Age of LLMs and Agentic AI*.
> Canon sources: Nonaka (1994); Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995); Nonaka & Konno (1998); Nonaka, Toyama & Konno (2000); Nonaka & von Krogh (2009); Polanyi (1966). Critiques: Gourlay (2006); Tsoukas (1996). KBV linkage: Grant (1996); Kogut & Zander (1992).

---

## 1. Purpose and provenance

Ikujiro Nonaka and Hirotaka Takeuchi's theory of organizational knowledge creation — most fully stated in the *Organization Science* article "A Dynamic Theory of Organizational Knowledge Creation" (Nonaka 1994) and the book *The Knowledge-Creating Company* (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995) — remains the canonical account of how organizations create knowledge. The theory was developed inductively from studies of Japanese innovation practice in the 1970s–1980s (Matsushita, Honda, Canon, NEC, Sharp) and was positioned as a deliberate break with the "information-processing" paradigm, in which the firm was conceived as a machine for processing inputs into outputs (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995, ch. 2). Nonaka & Takeuchi argued instead that organizations create knowledge through the continuous conversion of tacit and explicit knowledge, and that this conversion — amplified across levels of social aggregation — constitutes the wellspring of sustained innovation.

This brief reconstructs the model in full detail — the tacit/explicit distinction; the four SECI modes; the knowledge spiral; *ba*; the Matsushita Home Bakery case; the theory's known weaknesses; and its connection to the knowledge-based view of the firm — to anchor an investigation of whether the model still explains knowledge creation when much knowledge work is performed by large language models (LLMs) and agentic AI systems.

## 2. Tacit and explicit knowledge: the Polanyian foundation

The theory's load-bearing wall is Michael Polanyi's distinction between tacit and explicit knowledge. In *The Tacit Dimension* (Polanyi 1966), Polanyi argued that "we can know more than we can tell" (p. 4): a large part of what we know — how to ride a bicycle, recognize a face, or knead dough — resists full articulation in words or formal symbols. Tacit knowledge is personal, context-specific, and deeply embodied; it is acquired through practice and experience rather than instruction. Polanyi's point is not merely that some knowledge is *currently* unexpressed but that the tacit dimension is ineradicable: explicit knowledge always rests on a tacit substrate, and every act of knowing involves a "personal coefficient" that cannot be fully objectified (Polanyi 1958, 1966).

Nonaka & Takeuchi adopted this distinction but gave it a more operational, management-oriented form. They distinguished two dimensions of tacit knowledge (Nonaka 1994): a *technical* dimension — the informal, hard-to-pin-down skills and "know-how" captured in the craftsman's touch (what Gilbert Ryle (1949) called "knowing how"); and a *cognitive* dimension — mental models, schemata, beliefs, and perspectives that shape how individuals perceive and make sense of the world. Explicit knowledge, by contrast, is knowledge that can be codified and transmitted in formal, systematic language — documents, formulas, specifications, databases.

Two qualifications matter for the present project. First, Nonaka's definition of knowledge is deliberately non-cartesian: knowledge is "a dynamic human process of justifying personal belief toward the 'truth'" (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995, p. 58). The emphasis falls on *justification* — belief that has been warranted through social interaction and organizational intention — drawing on the social-constructionist tradition (Berger & Luckmann 1966) rather than on a purely representationalist epistemology. Second, Nonaka & von Krogh (2009) later refined the tacit/explicit distinction from a dichotomy into a *continuum*: tacit and explicit knowledge are mutually complementary, and knowledge moves along the continuum through processes of conversion, expression, and embodiment. This refinement was partly a response to critics who argued that Polanyi's position makes full externalization impossible (see §7).

## 3. The SECI model: four modes of knowledge conversion

The acronym SECI names the four modes through which tacit and explicit knowledge are converted into one another (Nonaka 1994; Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995, ch. 3). Each mode is defined by the direction of conversion:

| Mode | From → To | Characteristic process |
|------|-----------|------------------------|
| **Socialization** | tacit → tacit | Sharing experience; observation, imitation, apprenticeship |
| **Externalization** | tacit → explicit | Articulation through dialogue, metaphor, analogy, models |
| **Combination** | explicit → explicit | Reconfiguring, sorting, adding, synthesizing existing explicit knowledge |
| **Internalization** | explicit → tacit | Embodiment through learning by doing |

**Socialization** (tacit → tacit) is the sharing of tacit knowledge through shared experience. Because tacit knowledge cannot be transmitted in language alone, it must be acquired by co-participation in practice: apprentices learn a craft by watching and imitating a master; teams develop shared mental models through working together on-site. Nonaka & Takeuchi's canonical example is Honda's *tama dashi kai* ("brainstorming camps") — informal retreats at which engineers build the shared experience and mutual trust from which novel ideas emerge (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995, ch. 4). Socialization produces *sympathized knowledge* — a shared tacit base that grounds further conversion.

**Externalization** (tacit → explicit) is the articulation of tacit knowledge into explicit concepts — hypotheses, models, metaphors, analogies, and prototypes. Nonaka & Takeuchi regard this as the "quintessential" knowledge-creation process, because it is here that knowledge moves from the private and embodied into a communicable, shareable form. Externalization is triggered by dialogue and collective reflection, in which individuals express their implicit mental models in figurative language: metaphor first, then analogy, then a concrete model or concept. The Matsushita Home Bakery case (§6) is the archetype.

**Combination** (explicit → explicit) reconfigures existing explicit knowledge into new, more systematic knowledge: sorting, adding, combining, and categorizing concepts; producing documents, databases, and reports; holding meetings and exchanging messages. Combination is the mode most familiar to conventional information management — it is how an organization synthesizes scattered facts into a specification, a strategy document, or a product plan. Combination is facilitated by information technology and formal communication channels; it generates *systemic knowledge* whose value depends on the quality of the explicit material and the judgment applied in recombining it.

**Internalization** (explicit → tacit) is the embodiment of explicit knowledge into individual tacit knowledge — "learning by doing." When individuals read documents, study manuals, or rehearse procedures and then put them into practice, the explicit knowledge is absorbed, modified, and enriched with personal experience, becoming part of the individual's tacit repertoire. Internalization closes one loop of the spiral and prepares the ground for a new cycle of socialization at a higher level.

The four modes are not independent: they form a *conversion sequence* — socialization generates the shared tacit base that externalization can articulate; combination systematizes the resulting explicit knowledge; internalization embodies it; and the newly enriched tacit knowledge enables richer socialization. Knowledge creation is thus an *amplification* process: knowledge born in an individual is converted, articulated, and combined until it becomes organizational knowledge (Nonaka 1994).

## 4. The knowledge spiral

Nonaka's central dynamic claim is that knowledge creation follows an *upward spiral*, not a closed circle. The spiral results from the interaction of two dimensions (Nonaka 1994; Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995): the *epistemological* dimension of tacit–explicit conversion (SECI) and the *ontological* dimension of social aggregation — individual → group → organization → inter-organization. Each full SECI cycle does not return knowledge to its starting point; it escalates the level of social aggregation, so that knowledge created by an individual becomes group knowledge, then organizational knowledge, and eventually knowledge shared across organizational boundaries — "starting at the individual level and moving up through expanding communities of interaction" (Nonaka 1994, p. 20).

The spiral does not run by itself; Nonaka (1994) specifies five enabling conditions that organizations must cultivate. (1) *Organizational intention*: a strategic vision that provides the yardstick for justifying which beliefs count as knowledge. (2) *Autonomy*: self-organizing teams whose independence invites serendipitous, divergent knowledge. (3) *Fluctuation and creative chaos*: deliberate exposure to environmental turbulence that breaks routines and forces re-examination of fundamental assumptions. (4) *Redundancy*: overlapping information and intentional slack that create the shared vocabulary and surprise from which new combinations arise. (5) *Requisite variety*: internal diversity matching environmental complexity, achieved through flat, permeable structures.

The spiral also implies a particular theory of management. Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995) reject both "top-down" management (knowledge imposed downward from executives) and "bottom-up" management (knowledge bubbling up from frontline entrepreneurs). Their alternative is *middle-up-down* management: top management articulates visions and dreams; the frontline generates the concrete, context-rich knowledge of practice; and middle managers — the "knowledge producers" or "knowledge engineers" — synthesize the two, translating abstract vision into concrete concepts and back again. The organization best suited to this is the *hypertext organization* (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995; Nonaka, Toyama & Konno 2000), which layers a business-system, a project-team, and a knowledge-base stratum so that knowledge can move freely among them.

## 5. Ba: shared context for knowledge creation

Knowledge conversion does not occur in a vacuum; it requires a *ba* — a shared context in which knowledge is created, shared, and utilized. The concept, developed by Nonaka & Konno (1998) and formalized in Nonaka, Toyama & Konno (2000), derives from the Japanese philosopher Kitaro Nishida's notion of *basho* ("place"). Ba is not merely physical space but an existential "here and now" — a time-space nexus of relationships whose participants, through intention and commitment, supply the energy and quality that make knowledge creation possible. Knowledge is embedded in ba and is acquired through participation in it — ba is "a shared context in motion," continually recreated by the interactions it hosts (Nonaka, Toyama & Konno 2000).

Nonaka & Konno (1998) describe three kinds of ba, by medium. *Physical ba*: offices, meeting rooms, laboratories, and other co-located workplaces where face-to-face interaction can occur. *Virtual ba*: electronic space — email, teleconferencing, groupware, intranets — where interaction is mediated by information technology. *Mental ba*: shared ideals, values, experiences, and mental models — the cognitive common ground that makes meaningful interaction possible regardless of physical or virtual location. Effective knowledge creation typically draws on all three.

The 2000 formulation maps ba onto the SECI modes through two dimensions — the medium of interaction (face-to-face vs. virtual) and the level of interaction (individual vs. collective) — yielding four types: *originating ba* (individual, face-to-face), the existential site of socialization, where feelings and experiences are shared and trust develops; *dialoguing ba* (collective, face-to-face), the site of externalization, where dialogue and metaphor articulate mental models; *systemizing ba* (collective, virtual), the site of combination, where explicit knowledge is exchanged and stored in databases and groupware; and *exercising ba* (individual, virtual), the site of internalization, where knowledge is embodied through mentoring, simulation, and action. Leadership in the knowledge-creating company is therefore largely *ba-building*: sustaining the physical, virtual, and mental contexts in which the spiral can turn, and cultivating the knowledge assets that feed it (Nonaka, Toyama & Konno 2000).

## 6. The Matsushita Home Bakery: the archetype of externalization

The theory's signature case is the development of Matsushita Electric's Home Bakery bread-making machine in 1985, recounted in *The Knowledge-Creating Company* (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995, ch. 3). The product team's problem was that the machine's kneading mechanism could not reproduce hand-kneaded dough, yielding loaves with a burnt crust and undercooked, sticky center. The critical know-how — how to knead properly — was tacit, embodied in the hands of professional bakers and unarticulated even by the bakers themselves.

The software developer Ikuko Tanaka apprenticed herself to the head baker of the Osaka International Hotel. Through weeks of observation and practice she internalized the technique — and because she had embodied the skill herself, she was able to *name* it: the secret was "twist and stretch," a metaphor for the rhythmic kneading motion that develops the dough's gluten structure. This moment is the theory's canonical instance of externalization: tacit skill, acquired through socialization, articulated into an explicit, communicable concept via metaphor. The team then translated the metaphor into engineering: they designed ribs inside the bread pan so that the dough would be twisted and stretched against them as it rotated, mechanically replicating the baker's hands. Further cycles of prototyping and testing — internalization through learning by doing — refined the design, and the Home Bakery sold over a million units in its first year, creating the home-baking market.

Nonaka & Takeuchi use the case to make two theoretical points. First, externalization is the decisive conversion: the product breakthrough was the articulation of an unnameable skill, and it rested on prior socialization — Tanaka could only name the technique because she had first lived it. Second, the case shows the spiral at work: individual tacit knowledge (Tanaka's kneading skill) was externalized into a concept ("twist and stretch"), combined with engineering knowledge into a specification (the ribbed pan), internalized through prototyping, and ultimately institutionalized in a product that embodied organizational knowledge. The Home Bakery remains the standard against which any claim that a system can externalize tacit knowledge — including, for this project, an LLM-based system — must be tested.

## 7. Critiques and weak points

**Gourlay's critique.** The most systematic challenge is Stephen Gourlay's (2006) "Conceptualizing Knowledge Creation: A Critique of Nonaka's Theory." Gourlay argues, first, that the empirical basis is thin: the model rests almost entirely on case studies produced by the theorists or their associates, with little independent evidence that organizational knowledge creation proceeds through the four modes. Second, he challenges the *conversion* metaphor: it is doubtful that tacit knowledge can be converted into explicit knowledge at all, since Polanyi's position implies the tacit component of knowing is in principle inarticulable; conversion may conflate "knowing how" with "knowing that" (Ryle 1949). Third, the definition of knowledge as "justified true belief" is philosophically contentious, and the theory is vague about what distinguishes knowledge *creation* from recombination or problem-solving. Fourth, he questions the assumed sequence: there is little evidence that creation follows S→E→C→I, and new knowledge can plausibly arise without any conversion — through practice alone. Finally, he sees the theory as normative and hard to falsify: with broad enabling conditions and unspecified outcomes, almost any process can be retrofitted to it.

**The Polanyi interpretation dispute.** A related philosophical objection (Tsoukas 1996) holds that tacit and explicit knowledge are not poles between which knowledge can be shuttled but inseparably entangled aspects of knowing: explicit knowledge always rests on a tacit backdrop, making "externalization" as full articulation a category mistake; knowledge is inherently indeterminate, and codification transforms rather than transfers it. Nonaka & von Krogh (2009) responded by recasting the distinction as a continuum and reframing conversion as movement along that continuum — partial articulation that leaves a residual tacit surplus — while defending SECI's continuing relevance.

**Empirical support.** Independent empirical tests of SECI have produced partial and inconsistent results. Studies of knowledge-creation behavior in new-product-development teams, for example, find that some conversion modes predict knowledge creation while others do not, and that the model's effects are sensitive to context (Schulze & Hoegl 2008). Core constructs — especially ba and tacit knowledge — have proven hard to operationalize, and much quantitative work relies on self-reports of knowledge-sharing rather than measures of new knowledge. The theory's origins in Japanese firms of the 1980s also raise a cultural-specificity concern, though Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995) argued for universality, adducing Western cases (3M, GE, Hewlett-Packard).

**Linearity critique.** A recurrent charge is that SECI is typically rendered as a linear, quasi-sequential cycle (S→E→C→I), whereas real knowledge creation is messy, iterative, and dialectical: modes interleave, repeat, and skip; failure loops back into socialization; and much creation never completes a clean cycle. Nonaka and his co-authors have partially conceded this, emphasizing in later work that knowledge creation is a *synthesizing* process — the dialectical resolution of opposites (tacit/explicit, mind/body, individual/organization, order/chaos) rather than a mechanical sequence (Nonaka & Toyama 2003; Nonaka, Toyama & Konno 2000). The literature also notes the theory's thin treatment of power, politics, and conflict in knowledge processes, and its difficulty specifying when conversion produces *new* knowledge rather than rearranging existing knowledge.

## 8. The knowledge-based view of the firm

Nonaka & Takeuchi's theory is the dynamic complement to the knowledge-based view of the firm (KBV), which treats knowledge as the firm's most strategically significant resource. Two KBV statements matter here. Grant (1996) argues that the primary role of the firm is not to *create* knowledge — which he regards as fundamentally an individual achievement — but to *integrate* the specialized knowledge of its members. Because tacit knowledge is difficult to transfer and markets fail at moving it, firms exist as efficient institutions for integration, coordinating specialists through common knowledge (shared language and meaning), rules and directives, sequencing, routines, and group problem-solving. Grant's firm is an integrating mechanism; knowledge creation as such is largely exogenous to his account.

Kogut & Zander (1992) take a position closer to Nonaka's: the firm is "a social community specializing in the speed and efficiency in the creation and transfer of knowledge" (p. 384). They distinguish *information* (facts that can be transmitted without loss of meaning) from *know-how* (accumulated practical skill, largely tacit), and they argue that firms outperform markets at transferring tacit knowledge because they supply the shared identity, codes, language, and organizing principles that make such transfer possible. Their central construct, *combinative capabilities*, denotes the firm's ability to synthesize and apply existing and newly acquired knowledge to generate new applications — the capacity for recombination that drives firm growth. Firms grow by replicating their knowledge into new domains, and imitation is impeded by the tacit, causally ambiguous character of that knowledge.

The connection to SECI is direct. Combination is essentially combinative capability at the level of explicit knowledge (Kogut & Zander 1992), and the firm's "shared codes and identity" are a close cousin of ba's mental dimension — the shared context that makes knowledge conversion possible (Nonaka, Toyama & Konno 2000). Conversely, SECI supplies what Grant's (1996) integration-focused KBV lacks: a mechanism by which knowledge is actually *generated* inside firms rather than merely aggregated and applied. Where Grant sees individuals creating and firms integrating, Nonaka & Takeuchi see creation itself as a social, organizational achievement — knowledge that is born individual but becomes organizational through conversion in ba. The KBV provides the strategic stakes (knowledge as the inimitable, value-creating resource; firms as knowledge-based entities), while SECI provides the micro-dynamics. For this project, the KBV also frames the *design* stakes: if firms are knowledge-creating communities (Kogut & Zander 1992), then the introduction of AI agents into the community changes both the mechanisms of combination and the shared context in which conversion occurs.

## 9. Significance for this research project

This brief establishes the mechanics the rest of the paper will test. Five load-bearing claims follow from the theory: (1) knowledge creation is conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge, so the fate of the tacit dimension — including the possibility of *machine* tacit knowledge — is decisive (Polanyi 1966; Nonaka & von Krogh 2009); (2) externalization, the quintessential mode, is achievable only when a prior tacit base exists, which makes the Matsushita case the benchmark for any claim that LLMs can elicit or articulate tacit know-how (Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995); (3) creation escalates across ontological levels, so organizational memory and cross-agent knowledge flows matter as much as individual model capability (Nonaka 1994); (4) conversion requires ba — shared context in motion — which raises the question of what ba becomes when participants include agents (Nonaka, Toyama & Konno 2000); and (5) the model's known weak points — empirical thinness, the inarticulability objection, and the linearity problem — define the standards of evidence the paper must meet when extending SECI into the age of LLMs and agentic AI (Gourlay 2006; Tsoukas 1996).
